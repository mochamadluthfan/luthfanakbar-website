Research Note 01 · September 2026 · Energy transition · packaging & range

How much range do you get for a liter of car?

A fixed volume budget, split between battery, gasoline, and hydrogen storage, validated against a real vehicle, the Honda CR-V family, the only nameplate sold as a gasoline SUV, a hybrid, and a plug-in hydrogen fuel cell SUV on one platform.

Luthfan Akbar · luthfanakbar.com

Put a battery, a fuel tank, and a hydrogen tank in a straight fight over the same physical space in a car, and the outcome isn't close, but the reason why is more interesting than the result. This is a small model built to see exactly how much range a plug-in hybrid gives up, or gains, as you shift its storage volume between a battery pack and a tank, for both a gasoline range extender and a hydrogen fuel cell.

Vehicle: fixed 440 L installed-component budget, calibrated to the Honda CR-V platform. Range is total, electric-only plus extended, on one full charge and one full tank, over a synthetic mixed drive cycle. Mass is with three occupants aboard.

How to read it

At 0% the entire budget goes to battery, effectively a BEV with a dead engine or fuel cell riding along unused. At 100% the entire budget goes to fuel storage, with no battery at all. Everywhere in between is a real plug-in hybrid: some electric-only range, then a fuel-fed extension once the battery is depleted.

The dashed green line is what you'd get if you deleted the converter (engine, or fuel cell) entirely and gave 100% of the whole volume budget to battery instead: the pure-BEV ceiling for this exact packaging envelope.

What it shows

Gasoline wins by a wide and widening margin almost everywhere. At a quarter of the storage volume in the tank, close to what real plug-in hybrids on sale today actually use, the gasoline version already covers 824 km total, more than double the pure-battery ceiling. Push the split further toward fuel and the curve just keeps climbing, because gasoline's volumetric energy density so far exceeds what a car actually needs that there's essentially no natural ceiling within a realistic tank size.

Hydrogen never gets there. Even with the entire 440 L budget devoted to a 700 bar tank and no battery at all, the model tops out at 516 km, barely above what a well-executed pure-battery version of the same car already achieves with no fuel system at all. The reason isn't fuel-cell efficiency, which is actually good: a hydrogen fuel cell converts stored energy to motion more efficiently than a small gasoline engine does. The reason is storage. Compressed hydrogen, tank included, stores about 0.64 kWh per liter at today's technology; a gasoline tank stores about 7.2 kWh per liter. That roughly eleven-to-one gap in volumetric density is not something a ~1.5× efficiency advantage can make up for.

There's one narrow region worth naming: below about 3% tank share, hydrogen briefly out-ranges gasoline, because a fuel-cell stack physically packages smaller than an engine, generator, and exhaust system, leaving slightly more room for battery. It's a real effect, and it's also the only place hydrogen wins.

How the model was built

This isn't a spec-sheet comparison of unrelated cars. It's one fixed packaging envelope, swept continuously from all-battery to all-fuel, so the only thing that changes between any two points on the curve is the split itself.

  • Road load and consumption come from a synthetic drive cycle (45% urban, 25% suburban, 30% highway by distance, mean speed 48 km/h) run through a standard road-load equation: rolling resistance, aerodynamic drag, and inertia, with regenerative braking and a fixed auxiliary load.
  • Fuel mass is integrated along the route, not held constant: as the tank empties, the car gets lighter and consumption per kilometer drops accordingly, exactly as it does in reality.
  • Hardware is calibrated to the real Honda CR-V family: glider mass from the 2026 CR-V's published curb weight, engine mass from the actual L15B7 1.5T dressed weight, hydrogen storage density from the U.S. Department of Energy's own onboard-storage program record, and battery density from published LFP pack figures.
  • Validated, not just calibrated: reproducing the real production Honda CR-V e:FCEV inside this exact model, at the volume split that car actually uses, lands within 3% of its real EPA-rated range, on a model tuned to none of its numbers except fuel-cell efficiency.

The honest caveat: this is a compact-SUV-sized packaging envelope with a synthetic drive cycle, not a regulatory test. Treat the curve's shape and the qualitative conclusion, that gasoline's volumetric advantage over hydrogen is large and structural and not a matter of fuel-cell efficiency, as the finding, and the exact kilometer figures as good-to-a-few-percent estimates.
